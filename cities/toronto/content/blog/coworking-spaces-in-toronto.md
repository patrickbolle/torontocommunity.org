Most people pick a coworking space the way they pick a gym: closest one, cheapest plan, done. That works if all you need is a desk and Wi-Fi. But if you work alone, the space you pick is also your office social life. It decides who you say good morning to, who you eat lunch with, and who you complain about the coffee with.

I think that part matters more than the chair. So this guide is about choosing a Toronto coworking space for the people in it. Every space below comes from [the directory's coworking page](/coworking/).

## First, decide what you want from it

Before you tour anything, be honest about why you want a space. There are roughly three answers:

- **You want focus.** You need a quiet place that is not your kitchen. Community is a bonus.
- **You want colleagues.** You miss having people around. You want familiar faces and someone to eat lunch with.
- **You want your people.** You want a room full of folks who do work like yours, so you can trade advice, referrals and gossip.

The first answer can live almost anywhere. The second and third depend much more on the space.

## The kinds of coworking spaces in Toronto

### Spaces built around a shared mission

**[Centre for Social Innovation (CSI)](/coworking/#centre-for-social-innovation-(csi))** is the big one: Canada's largest social innovation community, with hot desks, dedicated desks and private offices at its Spadina hub. Nonprofits, artists and impact founders fill the room, and the events calendar is the real draw. If you work at a nonprofit, a social enterprise or anything near it, this is the closest thing to a ready-made peer group. Your desk neighbour might already know your funder.

### Small, boutique spaces

**[Adelaide House Coworking](/coworking/#adelaide-house-coworking)** is a boutique space in a converted garment-cutting warehouse in the Fashion District — tall ceilings, warm wood and a lounge that feels like home. Day passes are $40, and members get 24/7 access. Small spaces are where you learn everyone's name fast, which is the whole point if you want colleagues.

### Neighbourhood spaces

**[Acme Works](/coworking/#acme-works)** is at 229 Niagara St in the West End, a warm, dog-friendly house for freelancers, remote workers and small teams. The shared harvest tables are the heart of it, and regulars mention making actual friends here. If you live nearby, a space you can walk to is a space you will actually use. Use is what turns strangers into regulars.

**[Miraki Life Workspace](/coworking/#miraki-life-workspace)** is a creative studio and coworking space on Golden Ave in Roncesvalles, with 14-foot ceilings and street access. Day and evening passes, plus workshops and events, draw a mix of creatives, producers and remote teams. The evening pass matters if your workday starts late.

### Practical spaces

**[Workplace One](/coworking/#workplace-one)** runs several Toronto work centres, including its King East location at 340 King St E, a heritage brick-and-beam building near Corktown and the Distillery District. Hot desks sit alongside private offices and meeting rooms, pitched at professionals who want a quieter, well-run space.

If you are west of the city, **[ACE Coworking](/coworking/#ace-coworking)** in Oakville and **[Touchdown Coworking](/coworking/#touchdown-coworking)**, which serves Oakville and Mississauga, run much the same practical mix, with member networking on top. Touchdown's Crown & Community breakfast is a low-pressure way in.

### Big networks

**[Workhaus](/coworking/#workhaus)** is Canadian-owned, with a cluster of downtown locations — 901 King St W, 215 Spadina Ave and others — from hot desks to private offices. Shared spaces and a community-driven ethos aim to make a big-city workday feel less anonymous. A big network gives you flexibility. The trade-off is that bigger rooms can feel anonymous unless you show up to the events.

## Try a day pass before you commit

Do not sign a monthly plan off a tour. A tour shows you the space at its best, usually mid-morning with the light coming in. Ask if the space has a day pass or a trial day. Adelaide House lists one at $40, and Acme Works and Miraki both sell them. Then work there on a normal weekday, ideally twice.

While you are there, notice a few things:

- Do people talk to each other, or does everyone wear headphones all day?
- Is there a kitchen or common table where people actually eat together?
- Are there regular events, and do members go to them?
- Who is the community manager, and do they introduce you to anyone?

On a tour, ask what kind of work most members do. Ask what the last member event was. If nobody can remember, that tells you something.

## How to actually meet people at a coworking space

Paying for a desk does not make friends. Showing up does. These are the habits that work:

- **Pick fixed days.** Go Tuesday and Thursday every week. The same people tend to come on the same days, so you will start to see the same faces.
- **Sit in the open.** A closed office is great for focus and terrible for meeting anyone. Work from the shared tables at least some of the time.
- **Eat lunch in the kitchen.** It is the easiest place to start a conversation that is not about work.
- **Go to the events, even the awkward ones.** Event nights are where members who never talk at their desks finally do.
- **Ask one person one question.** "What are you working on?" is enough. Most people at coworking spaces are a bit lonely too.

This is the same rule I use for [making friends in Toronto](/blog/how-to-make-friends-in-toronto/) in general. Friendship comes from repetition. Go three times before you judge a place. The first day you are the new person. By the third week someone will save you a seat.

## If the space is quiet, bring your own people

Some spaces are great desks but quiet rooms. That is fine. You can pair a calm space with a group that meets in the evening. Toronto has a lot of [tech meetups](/tech-startup/), founder circles and dev meetups, and they are a good way to meet people who work like you do.
