There's a special kind of friendship that starts over a workbench. Someone sees you struggling with a jig, wanders over, and says, "Here, try it this way." Ten minutes later you're both covered in sawdust and talking about the next project.

That's what a makerspace gives you: tools you couldn't fit in an apartment, and people who love to make things. Toronto has a good mix, from a volunteer-run hackerspace to a shared shop with a welding bay to free repair nights at the library. How to pick one. All of these come from [the directory's maker spaces page](/maker-spaces/).

## If you want a hackerspace

**[HackLab.TO](/maker-spaces/#hacklab.to)** is a volunteer-run, not-for-profit hackerspace at 1677 St Clair Ave W, near the Stockyards. Members share 3D printers, a laser cutter, a darkroom, electronics benches and a shop full of tools. The membership is a mix of artists, programmers, web designers and hardware hackers, which is a good sign: you want a room where the person beside you is doing something you don't understand yet.

The best part for newcomers: [HackLab](https://hacklab.to/) holds an open house every Tuesday from 6pm, and the site says newcomers are welcome. Drop by and look around before you commit. Membership itself takes a little time. You visit a couple of open houses, ask two members to sponsor you, then wait through a three-day comment period. That sounds fussy, but it's how the space stays friendly instead of anonymous.

HackLab also runs Junk Independence Day, an e-waste swap where people bring old electronics and take home someone else's. It's a good first event if you don't have a project yet.

## If you want a full shop

**[Site 3 CoLaboratory](/maker-spaces/#site-3-colaboratory)** is a 2,000 square foot shop at Ossington and Bloor, with woodworking, metalworking, welding, electronics, plastics and 3D printing. It is volunteer-run and a registered Ontario non-profit, and it describes its purpose as a four-step cycle: create, display, teach, inspire.

[Site 3](https://www.site3.ca/) opens its doors to the public every Thursday from 7 to 10pm. That open house is the easiest way in. Membership is $60 a month plus HST, with a two-month minimum, and new members start on limited shop hours before earning 24/7 access.

The calendar is worth a look too. The Toronto Electronics Meetup meets there on the second Wednesday of the month, the Toronto chapter of TOOOL (lockpicking) meets on the first Wednesday, and a fibre-craft night runs on the third Wednesday. Pick the night that matches your curiosity.

## If you're into media art

**[InterAccess](/maker-spaces/#interaccess)** is a media arts centre and studio at 32 Lisgar St, with a gallery, equipment rentals and a workshop program. Members get tool training on the laser cutter, mill and lathe, plus access to electronics and digital fabrication gear. It leans art-school rather than garage: the exhibitions are part of the point, and the workshops are taught by working artists.

If you want to make things that blink, move or respond, and you want to show them, this is the place. [InterAccess](https://www.interaccess.org/) lists its tool trainings and workshops on its site.

## If you sew

**[Shoppe & Tailor Studios](/maker-spaces/#shoppe-%26-tailor-studios)** is a queer-positive textile maker space and rental sewing studio at 163 Sterling Rd, Unit 167. It has domestic and industrial machines, fitting rooms and a studio collective of small fashion businesses. You can book lessons or workshops in tailoring, alterations, repairs and upcycling.

This is the one to pick if your project is a garment rather than a shelf. The studio collective means you're working beside people who sew for a living, which is the fastest way to learn.

## If you just need a tool or a fix

You don't need a membership to start making.

**[Toronto Tool Library](/maker-spaces/#toronto-tool-library)** lets you borrow tools instead of buying them. It has been lending since 2012 from 192 Spadina Ave, near Kensington Market, and members can take tools home for up to seven days. It also runs hands-on workshops in tool basics, sewing and leathercraft. Borrow a circular saw for a weekend, then take the class before you cut.

**[Repair Café Toronto](/maker-spaces/#repair-caf%C3%A9-toronto)** runs free community events where neighbours help neighbours learn to repair household items. [Repair Café Toronto](https://repaircafetoronto.ca/) lists small appliances, computers, electronics, clothes, jewellery and books among the things its volunteer fixers take on. Events happen at partner locations across the city, so check the calendar before you haul a kettle across town. Watch a fixer work and you'll learn a skill too.

## Other places to make things

- **[Mischief Makers](/maker-spaces/#mischief-makers)** is a neighbourhood makerspace at 247 Coxwell Ave in the Upper Beaches. It runs workshops, clubs and craft meets with an eco-friendly, reuse-first bent, and it's explicitly queer-owned, LGBTQ2S+ friendly and neuro-affirming. Good for families and for anyone who wants a smaller, warmer room than a full shop.
- **[Burnhamthorpe Library Makerspace](/maker-spaces/#burnhamthorpe-library-makerspace)** in Mississauga has sewing machines, button makers, Cricut cutters, computers and a 3D printer. Library makerspaces are the cheapest way to try a tool before you buy one.
- **[Make It! at Pierre Berton Resource Library](/maker-spaces/#make-it!-at-pierre-berton-resource-library)** in Woodbridge has a laser cutter, Cricut, sublimation printer and 3D printer, with workshops and open making for all ages.

## How to fit in at a makerspace

New makers often worry they'll look clumsy. Everyone in the room was a beginner once, and most of them remember it.

- **Go to the open night or a class first.** It's the easiest way to meet people. HackLab's Tuesday open house and Site 3's Thursday one both exist for exactly this.
- **Bring a small project.** "I'm trying to build a shelf" starts more conversations than "I'm just looking."
- **Ask for help.** Makers love to show off a technique. Let them.

Then go back. The third visit is when people start asking about your project. That rule is the core of my guide to [making friends in Toronto](/blog/how-to-make-friends-in-toronto/).
